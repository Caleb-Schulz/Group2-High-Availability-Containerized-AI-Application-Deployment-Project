Briar Rose

Once upon a time there lived a king and a queen who said every day, “Oh, if only we had a child!” but they never had one. Then it happened one day, while the queen was sitting in her bath, that a crab crawled out of the water onto the floor and said to her, “Your wish shall soon be fulfilled. Before a year passes, you shall give birth to a daughter.”

What the crab said actually came true, and the queen gave birth to a girl who was so beautiful that the king was beside himself with joy. He decided to hold a great feast. He invited not only his relatives, friends, and acquaintances, but also the wise women so that they would be kind and favorably disposed to the child. Now, there were thirteen wise women in his kingdom, but since he had only twelve golden plates from which they could eat, one of them had to be left out.

The feast was celebrated with great splendor, and when it came to an end, the wise women bestowed their miraculous gifts upon the child. One gave her virtue, the other beauty, the third wealth, and so on with everything desirable in the world. Just as eleven of them had finished making their pronouncements, the thirteenth suddenly entered. She wanted to avenge herself for not having been invited, and without greeting or looking at anyone, she called out in a loud voice, “In her fifteenth year the king’s daughter shall prick herself with a spindle and fall down dead.” Without saying another word, she turned around and left the hall.

Everyone was terrified. Then the twelfth wise woman stepped forward. She still had her wish, and though she could not undo the evil sentence, she could soften it. So she said, “It shall not be a real death, but only a deep sleep lasting one hundred years into which the princess will fall.”

The king, who wanted to save his dear child from this misfortune, issued an order that all the spindles in the entire kingdom were to be burned. Meanwhile, the gifts of the wise women were fulfilled in every way on the girl: she was so beautiful, well-behaved, kind, and clever that everyone who saw her could not help but love her.

It happened that on the very day she turned fifteen, the king and queen were not at home, and the maiden was left all alone in the castle. So she wandered about all over the place, inspecting rooms and chambers just as she pleased. Finally, she came to an old tower. She climbed up the narrow winding stairs until she reached a little door. In the lock was a rusty key, and when she turned it, the door sprang open.

There, in a little room, sat an old woman with a spindle, diligently spinning her flax. “Good day, old mother,” said the king’s daughter. “What are you doing there?”

“I am spinning,” said the old woman, nodding her head.

“What kind of thing is that rattling around so merrily?” asked the maiden, and she took the spindle and wanted to spin as well. No sooner had she touched the spindle than the magic spell took effect, and she pricked her finger with it.

The very moment she felt the prick, she fell down upon the bed that stood there, and lay in a deep sleep. And this sleep spread over the entire castle. The king and queen, who had just returned home and entered the great hall, began to fall asleep, and with them the entire royal court. The horses also fell asleep in the stalls, the dogs in the yard, the pigeons on the roof, the flies on the wall. Even the fire flickering on the hearth became still and fell asleep. The roast stopped sizzling, and the cook, who was just about to pull the kitchen boy’s hair because he had done something wrong, let him go and fell asleep. The wind died down, and on the trees in front of the castle not a single leaf stirred.

All around the castle a thorny hedge began to grow. Every year it grew higher and higher, until it finally surrounded the entire castle and grew right over it, so that nothing of it could be seen, not even the flag on the roof. Throughout the country the legend of the beautiful sleeping Briar Rose spread, for that was what the princess was called. From time to time princes came and tried to force their way through the hedge into the castle. But they could not succeed, for the thorns held together firmly as if they had hands, and the young men got caught in them, could not free themselves, and died a miserable death.

After many, many years, another prince came to the country and heard an old man tell of the thorny hedge. He said that a castle was supposed to stand behind it, in which a wonderfully beautiful princess named Briar Rose had been sleeping for a hundred years, and with her the king, queen, and the entire royal court. The old man also knew from his grandfather that many princes had already come and tried to break through the thorny hedge, but they had remained stuck in it and had died a pitiful death.

Then the young prince said, “I am not afraid. I shall go and see the beautiful Briar Rose.” The good old man tried as best he could to talk him out of it, but the prince would not listen to his words.

Now, the hundred years had just elapsed, and the day had arrived when Briar Rose was to wake up again. When the prince approached the thorny hedge, it was nothing but large, beautiful flowers that opened up by themselves, let him pass through unharmed, and then closed together behind him like a hedge.

In the castle yard he saw the horses and spotted hunting dogs lying asleep, and on the roof sat the pigeons with their little heads tucked under their wings. When he entered the palace, the flies were sleeping on the wall, the cook in the kitchen was still holding up his hand as if he wanted to grab the boy, and the maid was sitting in front of the black chicken that was to be plucked.

He walked farther and in the hall saw the entire court lying asleep, and up by the throne lay the king and queen. Then he moved on, and everything was so quiet that he could hear his own breath. Finally, he came to the tower and opened the door to the small room where Briar Rose was sleeping.

There she lay, and she was so beautiful that he could not take his eyes off her. He leaned over and gave her a kiss. As soon as he touched her with his lips, Briar Rose opened her eyes, woke up, and looked at him in a friendly way.

Then they went down together, and the king woke up, and the queen, and the entire court, and they all looked at one another in amazement. The horses in the courtyard stood up and shook themselves. The hunting dogs jumped up and wagged their tails. The pigeons on the roof pulled their little heads out from under their wings, looked around, and flew into the fields. The flies on the walls crawled on. The fire in the kitchen flared up, flickered, and cooked the meat. The roast began to sizzle again, and the cook gave the boy a slap on the ear so hard that he cried out, and the maid finished plucking the chicken.

And then the wedding of the prince and Briar Rose was celebrated in all magnificence, and they lived happily until their deaths.
