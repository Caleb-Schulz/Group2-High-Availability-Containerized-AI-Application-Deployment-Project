The Worn-Out Dancing Shoes

There was once a king who had twelve daughters, each one more beautiful than the next. They slept together in a single room where their beds stood side by side, and in the evening when they went to bed, the king locked the door and bolted it shut. Yet every morning when he unlocked the room, he saw that their shoes had been danced to pieces, and nobody could figure out how this happened.

At last the king issued a proclamation declaring that whoever could discover where the princesses danced at night would have his choice of one of them for his wife and would become king after his death. However, whoever presented himself and did not find out the truth within three days and three nights would forfeit his life.

Before long a prince arrived and offered to undertake the venture. He was well received, and when evening came, he was led to a chamber directly adjoining the bedchamber of the princesses. His bed was made there, and he was to watch where they went and danced. To ensure they could do nothing in secret, the chamber door was left open. But the prince's eyes grew heavy, and he fell asleep. When he awoke in the morning, all twelve pairs of shoes had holes in the soles from dancing. The second and third nights were no different, and the prince was beheaded without mercy. Many others came after him and attempted the venture, but they all lost their lives.

Now it happened that a poor soldier, who had received a wound and could no longer serve in the army, found himself on the road to the city where this king lived. Along the way he met an old woman, who asked him where he was going.

"I hardly know myself," he replied, and then added in jest, "I'd like to find out where the princesses dance their shoes to pieces and eventually become king."

"That is not so difficult," said the old woman. "All you have to do is refuse the wine that will be brought to you in the evening, and pretend to fall fast asleep."

With that, she gave him a little cloak and said, "When you put this on, you will become invisible, and then you will be able to follow the twelve girls wherever they go."

Once the soldier had received this good advice, he took the matter seriously, took heart, and went straight to the king to offer himself as a suitor. He was received just as graciously as the others had been, and royal garments were given to him. When night fell, he was led to the antechamber. Just as he was preparing to go to bed, the eldest princess entered and brought him a goblet of wine. But he had tied a small sponge under his chin, let the wine pour into it without swallowing a single drop, and then lay down. After a little while he began to snore loudly, as if he were sleeping deeply.

When the twelve princesses heard this, they laughed, and the eldest said, "He, too, could have spared his life!"

Then they arose, opened wardrobes and boxes, took out splendid gowns, adorned themselves before their mirrors, skipped about, and prepared for the dance. Only the youngest sister said, "I don't know why, but you are all so happy, while I feel quite uneasy. A misfortune will surely happen to us."

"You goose," said the eldest, "you are always afraid! Have you forgotten how many princes have watched us in vain? The soldier was given a sleeping draft anyway, and the fool won't wake up."

When they were all dressed, they checked on the soldier, but he kept his eyes closed and didn't move, so they felt completely safe. The eldest walked over to her bed and knocked on it. Immediately it sank into the ground, and one after another they climbed down through the opening, with the eldest leading the way. The soldier, who had seen everything, did not hesitate: he put on his little cloak and went down behind the youngest princess. Halfway down the stairs, he stepped lightly on the train of her gown.

She was startled and cried out, "What is that? Who is tugging at my gown?"

"Don't be so foolish," said the eldest. "You just caught it on a hook."

At the bottom of the stairs they stepped out into a marvelous avenue of trees, where all the leaves were made of silver, glittering and gleaming. The soldier thought, "I must take a token with me," and snapped off a branch, which made a loud crack.

The youngest cried out again, "All is not right! Didn't you hear that crack?"

"That is only a salute of joy from our princes because we have freed them so quickly," said the eldest.

Next they came to an avenue where the leaves were pure gold, and finally to a third where they were gleaming diamonds. From each tree the soldier snapped off a branch, and every time it cracked, the youngest princess shuddered with fear, though the eldest insisted it was only joy gunshots.

They walked on until they arrived at a large lake. Twelve handsome princes sat waiting in twelve little boats, and each took one of the princesses with him. The soldier sat down in the boat with the youngest.

The prince rowing said, "I don't know why, but the boat is much heavier today than usual. I must row with all my strength to move it."

"It must be the warm weather," said the youngest. "I feel very hot too."

On the opposite bank stood a magnificent, brightly lit castle, from which came the cheerful music of horns and trumpets. They entered the castle, and each prince danced with his beloved. The soldier danced along invisibly, and whenever a princess held a goblet of wine, he drank it dry just as she brought it to her mouth, which terrified the youngest even more, though the eldest always silenced her fears.

They danced until three in the morning, when all their shoes were worn through with holes, and they had to stop. The princes rowed them back across the water, and this time the soldier sat in the boat with the eldest. On the bank they bade their princes farewell, and the princesses climbed back up the hidden stairs. The soldier ran ahead of them, lay down in his bed, and when the twelve tired sisters came up, he was snoring so loudly that they all heard him.

"We are safe from him," they said, took off their beautiful dresses, put them away, placed their worn-out dancing shoes under their beds, and lay down to sleep.

The soldier did not report anything the next morning, wanting to witness the wonders again. He went along on the second and third nights, and everything happened just as before: they danced until their shoes fell apart. On the third night, the soldier took away a golden goblet as a further token.

When the hour came on the next day to give his answer, he hid the three branches and the goblet beneath his cloak and stepped before the king. The twelve sisters stood behind the door, listening to what he would say.

The king asked, "Where did my twelve daughters dance their shoes to pieces at night?"

The soldier answered, "In an underground palace with twelve princes."

He took out the three branches and the golden goblet, laying them before the king. The king summoned his daughters and asked whether the soldier had spoken the truth. When they saw they had been betrayed and that denying it would do no good, they confessed everything.

Then the king asked the soldier which one he wanted for his wife.

He replied, "I am no longer young, so give me the eldest."

Their wedding was celebrated that very day, and the kingdom was promised to the soldier upon the king's death.
